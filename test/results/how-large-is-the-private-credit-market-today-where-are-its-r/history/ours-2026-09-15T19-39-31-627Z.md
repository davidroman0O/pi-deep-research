# Assess the current size, leverage profile, and loss concentrations of the private credit market and quantify how a 2008-style rapid rate shock would transmit losses through it, to inform institutional allocation and risk-management decisions on private credit exposure.

**Research date:** 2026-09-15 · **Sources analyzed:** 9 · **Evidence records:** 60 · **Verified claims:** 60

---

## Executive Summary

Private credit now spans ~$3.5 trillion AUM, but its profile is structurally fragile to a 2008-style rapid rate shock. Borrowers are near-exclusively floating-rate, single-B rated, with mean interest coverage of ~2.0x versus ~2.7x for leveraged loans; over one-third of comparable firms already carry unsustainable coverage, and PIK usage has doubled since 2019, masking rather than resolving stress. Fund-level leverage has grown sharply, with ~$300 billion in bank commitments concentrated among a few sponsors. The strongest evidence points to faster, deeper cash-flow damage than public credit, where only ~29% of debt is variable-rate. The biggest uncertainty is the unquantified default-rate response, plus missing data on sector/borrower concentration, maturity walls, and systemic propagation. Recommendation: underwrite private credit for materially higher loss severity under rate shocks and source concentration, maturity, and regulatory data before sizing exposure.

## Market Size, Composition, and Growth Trajectory

## Market Size, Composition, and Growth Trajectory

The global private credit market reached US$3.5 trillion in AUM as of December 2025, per the ACC/AIMA "Financing the Economy 2025" report with Houlihan Lokey [1]. The estimate draws on a survey of 49 managers overseeing US$2.1 trillion [1]. Earlier vintages cite >US$1.5 trillion (2023–2025) [2] and >US$1.7 trillion, rivaling high-yield [3]; the ~2x gap is unreconciled, likely reflecting vintage and scope differences — the exposure base carries definitional uncertainty. AUM grew at a 10-year CAGR of 15.2% [3]; deployment reached US$592.8 billion in 2024, up 78% [1]. Dry powder is approximately US$400 billion, though single-sourced with no stated date or methodology [3].

| Period | Global private credit AUM |
|---|---|
| 2008–2012 | US$200–300B [2] |
| 2013–2017 | US$500–700B [2] |
| 2018–2022 | US$1,000–1,200B [2] |
| 2025–2027 (projection) | US$2T+ [2] |

Composition skews closed-ended (~80%), with ~20% semi-liquid vehicles using liquidity-management tools [1]; Europe holds close to 30% of AUM [1]. Six strategies — direct lending, mezzanine, unitranche, specialty finance, distressed/opportunistic, venture debt — are identified, but no quantitative split is available [2]. Direct-lending spreads have compressed from 600+ to 500–550 bps as capital inflows intensified [3].

## Leverage Profile at Fund and Borrower Level

Public BDC leverage — debt to total assets — rose from ~40% in 2017 to 53% in 2024, above the 42% nonbank average [4]. The NAV-facility market stood at ~$100B in 2024 [6], projected to reach $600B by 2030 [6]. NAV facilities borrow against portfolio NAV; breaching the prescribed LTV triggers partial or full repayment [6], with LTV limits declining as residual equity is sold (inference — no direct source). Usage splits ~80% portfolio-company support and 20% distributions [6], typically after commitments are drawn or reserves exhausted, sometimes blended with subscription lines (inference — no direct source).

| | Subscription line | NAV facility |
|---|---|---|
| Collateral | Undrawn LP commitments [7] | Existing fund assets [7] |
| Lifecycle use | Earlier, funding acquisitions [7] | Later, once most capital is drawn [7] |

Borrower-level leverage sits below its peak: new US first-lien deals run ~5.0x EBITDA, ~0.5x under the 2021–22 high of ~5.5x and slightly lower in Europe [9]. Interest coverage of 2.2x–2.4x is up ~0.2x–0.3x from 2023 troughs [9], with existing portfolios showing ~0.25x–0.5x deleveraging and ~0.25x better coverage [9]. Documentation remains borrower-friendly — cov-lite, PIK toggles, synthetic PIK, portability — into 2H25 [9]. Confidence is high (0.85–0.92); cov-lite prevalence is only qualitative, and whether NAV facilities count toward LPA leverage limits remains contested between ILPA and some GPs.

## Rate-Shock Transmission, Borrower Stress, and Historical Calibration

## Rate-Shock Transmission, Borrower Stress, and Historical Calibration

Private credit's transmission channel is direct: direct lending loans are near-exclusively floating-rate, priced over SOFR [4], versus only ~29% variable-rate debt among high-yield issuers [5]. The 2022 cycle showed the mechanics — all-in yields rose immediately and mechanically with each Fed hike, transmitting higher costs within days [4] and lifting average BDC return on equity to 12.0% [4]. Borrower stress is accumulating: over one-third of comparable firms now show unsustainable interest coverage [5], and PIK interest's share of BDC income has doubled since 2019, masking rather than resolving burdens that compound if underperformance persists [5]. Sponsor support and lender relationships partially mitigate these risks [5], though a stagflation scenario could generate a default surge [5].

Historical calibration: Moody's middle-market default rates ran 1.8–3.2% (2010–2023) versus 1.1–2.4% for broadly syndicated loans [4], likely understating true direct-lending defaults [4] — private credit borrowers are smaller, more highly leveraged, and more rate-sensitive [4]. Two caveats: the evidence base does not quantify default responses to a specific SOFR spike, and maturity-wall data remain an external gap — the sole adjacent point (4x+ debt multiples above the historical median in 2H25) does not address refinancing needs.

## Systemic Interconnectedness, Loss Propagation, and Regulatory Visibility

Bank–fund linkages are the only quantified transmission channel in the evidence base, and they remain modest relative to fund assets; estimates carry high confidence (0.90–0.92). As of 2023, banks in the FR Y-14 sample had extended roughly $300 billion in loan commitments to PE/PC funds and affiliated fund-level entities [7], about 14 percent of their total NBFI commitments, up from about 1 percent in 2013 [7]. Fund-level borrowing rose from below 0.2 percent of PE/PC AUM in 2013 to 1.8 percent in 2023 [7]. Concentration is notable: a few sponsors hold hundreds of fund-level loans each, together accounting for about one-third of aggregate commitments [7]. The industry remains far less levered than banks, whose deposits finance roughly 75 percent of assets [7]. Regulatory visibility is partial: ILPA maintains that NAV-based facilities constitute fund-level leverage and should count against borrowing limits, whereas some GPs exclude the SPV from LPA leverage caps [6]. No evidence in the claim set quantifies insurance, pension, or CLO transmission channels; external data (BDC filings, Cliffwater, Preqin, NAIC/Form PF) are required to complete those dimensions, leaving systemic loss magnitude unquantified.
